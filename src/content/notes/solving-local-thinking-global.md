The best problems I've worked on were the ones I could almost see from my window.

For a while I chased ideas because they sounded impressive. AI, AR, real-time video: the kind of projects that look great in a screenshot. I don't regret any of them. But the projects that changed how I think all started with a smaller question. Who is struggling with this right now, and what would make their day easier?

Campipal started there, with students and the everyday mess of campus life. Blail started with something even simpler: email is still awkward when your hands are busy, so I built an email app you can use hands-free. None of it was world-changing. All of it was real, and real is what taught me.

The restaurant websites taught me the most. Kenz Sushi in Algiers has a menu people want to see and tables people want to book, and their guests do it on a phone, in French or English. Yulmaz runs a restaurant and a catering service under one name, for customers who read Arabic as well as English. The 11th Floor in South Africa has won awards for its rooftop and needed a website that felt like being there. Three countries, three different briefs, one pattern underneath: a good business that customers need to find, understand and book, fast.

The research backs up what those owners already knew. Surveys by OpenTable and TouchBistro keep landing in the same place: well over 80% of diners look at a restaurant's menu online before they go. If the menu isn't there, or it's a blurry photo of a PDF, part of the room is already lost. That isn't really a tech problem. It's a business problem that technology happens to be good at solving.

What surprised me is how well local solutions travel. The things I built for Algiers — a language switch, WhatsApp one tap away, a booking button that never leaves the screen — made just as much sense for a rooftop in South Africa. Language, trust and speed on a mobile network look different in every city, but the need underneath is the same everywhere.

So this is the rule I'm writing down for myself. Start close. Talk to the person who actually has the problem. Build the smallest thing that makes it go away, and build it properly. Don't design for "global" in the abstract. Design for one real person, then notice how many other people turn out to be just like them.

Good engineering travels well. I just have to start it at home.
